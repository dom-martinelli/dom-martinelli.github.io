FDA-authorized AI devices

The FDA has authorized a long and growing list of AI medical devices. This interactive registry lets anyone explore that list, and shows what it never records: whether a device was shown to help patients.

The question

What does the FDA's public list of AI-enabled medical devices tell us, and what evidence about patient benefit is missing from it?

Why it matters

Hospitals, clinicians, and patients may assume that an FDA-authorized AI device has been proven to work in patient care. Most of these devices reach the market by showing they resemble a device already sold, which does not require a new clinical trial. The official list records who made a device and when it was cleared, but not the clinical evidence behind it.

What I did

  • Downloaded the FDA's official list of AI-enabled medical devices and built a single self-contained web page around it.
  • Added search and filters by device, company, specialty, clearance pathway, and year, with charts that update as you filter.
  • Linked each submission number to its record in the FDA database.
  • Summarized published peer-reviewed studies of this list to show, in aggregate, what the authorization summaries report about clinical validation, with each figure cited to its source.

What I found

  • Nearly all devices on the list reached the market through the 510(k) pathway, which asks about similarity to an existing device rather than new clinical evidence.
  • Published reviews of the authorization summaries report that prospective real-world studies, demographic details of validation populations, and linked peer-reviewed publications are rare.

Limits

  • The evidence summary comes from other researchers' published studies of the list as a whole, not from per-device review.
  • A snapshot of the FDA list at one download date; it does not update automatically.
