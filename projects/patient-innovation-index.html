Is there a patient-involvement citation advantage?

thirteen text signals of patient involvement, and what happens to the citation effect once the comparison group is right

question

Papers that report patients as partners are cited more than random PubMed papers. Is that because of the involvement, or because of the kind of literature those papers live in?

what came out
  • against 16,161 random PubMed papers the 553 papers with a detected report are cited 70% higher unadjusted (median RCR 1.113 against 0.655), 54% reweighted to their years, and 28% in median regression with year, log team size, article type and open access.
  • against the right control group the effect disappears. the 12,222 unflagged papers retrieved by the same search have a median RCR of 1.141, slightly above the flagged ones; adjusted, the papers reporting involvement sit 8% below them, interval -19 to 3.
  • the unflagged corpus is itself 52% above random PubMed papers. so the advantage belongs to the involvement-vocabulary literature as a whole, not to reporting partnership or organization support.
  • the adjusted estimate depends on the estimator: 28% in median regression, 8% under coarsened exact matching with an interval from -6 to 24. open access alone moves the year-adjusted estimate from 56% to 37%; 73% of the flagged papers are open access against 25% of random ones.
  • only six of thirteen signals identify real involvement against hand coding. patient author reached 9 of 9; self-report instruments, self-experimentation and patient-controlled data reached 0 of 10.
  • the draft index that summed all 13 signals flagged 9,442 papers and gave 6% in both regression and matching. the validated set is one seventeenth of that size, and it is the one the claim rests on.
calls made
  • the comparison group is the whole finding. a single random control confounds the involvement with the literature it sits in, which is what produced the earlier and much larger estimate.
  • signals were kept or dropped on measured precision against hand coding, not on whether they sounded like involvement. seven of thirteen were dropped.
  • relative citation ratio as the outcome, not raw counts, so each paper is normalised to its own co-citation field.
  • both the regression and the matching estimate are reported, because they disagree and the disagreement is the result.
  • full text of the 7,969 open-access papers read as a robustness check, since titles and abstracts miss involvement reported in acknowledgements.
what broke
  • case-folding bug: the scorer lowercased the text but kept capitalised patterns, so every acronym signal could never fire.
  • substring matching on organisation names produced false hits on unrelated institutions.
  • the collective-author signal was tagging research consortia as patient collectives, which mislabeled a headline signal in the draft.
  • once patterns went case-insensitive, a bare sample-size notation started matching results-section text.
  • a figure and its signal table disagree because they came from different pipeline runs. reconciling to one canonical run is open.
