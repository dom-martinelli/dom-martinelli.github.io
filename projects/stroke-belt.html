question

Within the Stroke Belt, is high blood pressure more common where obesity is, and are diagnosed adults less likely to be on medication where the Black population share is larger?

what i did
  • joined CDC PLACES tract estimates to ACS demographics and Census TIGER geometries across the Stroke Belt states, 18,063 tracts after cleaning.
  • fitted a standardised linear model for medication coverage among diagnosed adults on the share of Black residents and the share insured, then tested the coefficient of interest against 300 label permutations rather than against a normal theory p-value.
  • fitted a second model for hypertension prevalence itself on obesity, median income, share insured and median age, dropping the behavioural measures that are collinear with obesity.
  • tested a third, temporal question separately: whether tracts with lower insurance saw a larger change in hypertension prevalence from 2019 to 2021, across 11,718 tracts.
what came out
  • a higher Black population share goes with lower medication coverage among adults already diagnosed, holding insurance constant. Standardised coefficient -0.106, permutation p = 0.017.
  • that model explains almost nothing. R-squared is 0.0008, so race and insurance together account for under a tenth of one percent of the variation in who is on treatment. The direction is real and reproducible; the two variables are nowhere near an account of the outcome.
  • obesity is the dominant predictor of hypertension prevalence, standardised coefficient +2.86 with permutation p below 0.000001, in a model reaching R-squared 0.43. Median age comes second at +2.26 and median income runs the other way at -1.94.
  • lower insurance coverage tracks a larger rise in hypertension prevalence between 2019 and 2021, slope -0.257, permutation p = 0.001.
calls made
  • widened from one metro to the Stroke Belt states. the cleaned Chicago sample was a tiny slice of US tracts, and the hypertension burden is documented across the region.
  • medication coverage among adults already diagnosed as the treatment outcome, not recorded prevalence. low recorded prevalence can mean less testing rather than less disease.
  • interquartile rule for flagging high-burden tracts, not z-scores. tract percentages are bounded and right-skewed.
  • collinear behavioural measures left out of the second model. inactivity, smoking and short sleep are multicollinear with obesity, the predictor of interest.
what broke
  • tract IDs kept losing their leading zero when read as numbers, which breaks joins for every state whose FIPS code starts with one, with no error raised.
  • the first model cell was committed in an erroring state after the medication column was renamed.
  • the source column name for the treatment measure implied a share of the whole tract population rather than of diagnosed adults. renamed before analysis.
what i tried

first version was Chicago tracts with HOLC redlining grades. too few tracts survived cleaning, so the team moved to Stroke Belt states and dropped the redlining layer.

so what
  • the two headline results are the same analysis at opposite extremes, which is why they belong on one page. A significant coefficient with R-squared 0.0008 and a significant coefficient with R-squared 0.43 would be reported identically by a p-value alone. Reporting the effect without the fit is how an area-level disparity gets written up as though it were an explanation.
